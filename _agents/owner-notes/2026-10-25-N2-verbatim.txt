  Analysis 2   Short answer: The table is internally consistent as a model ranking, but it shouldn’t be presented as a conventional ranking of NFL positional value or salary. It puts the kicker above RB1 and WR2, which is hard to defend by either of those standards.
What the table says
Taking its “wins” figures at face value, the order is simply the listed order:
QB → CB1 → Edge 1 → CB2 → WR1 → Edge 2 → tackles → DT1 → nickel CB → strong safety → MIKE → free safety → guards/center → WILL → TE1 → DT2 → kicker → RB1 → WR2 → WR3 → RB2 → TE2 → SAM.
That can be a valid output from a particular model. But the numbers don’t explain enough to establish that they measure real-world value: there’s no source or method for converting ratings into wins, and the salary comparisons depend on whether you mean average player pay, starter pay, top contracts, or cap spending.
Where it conflicts with conventional logic and salary
* WR2 below kicker and RB1: That’s the clearest mismatch. The table gives WR2 0.60 wins, versus 0.72 for the kicker and 0.67 for RB1. Yet the top-paid WR contracts are far above top-paid contracts at either position: in 2025, Ja’Marr Chase’s average annual value was $40.3M, compared with Saquon Barkley’s $20.6M; 2025 kicker/punter franchise tags were $6.218M, versus $13.437M for running backs. Those are different salary measures, but they illustrate why the table’s kicker-over-WR2 order is not a typical salary ranking. NFL.com’s 2025 contract roundup · 2025 franchise-tag figures
* Kicker’s rank needs qualification: A kicker can decide a close game, so the position matters. But “kicking only” doesn’t by itself establish that the starter is more valuable overall than a regular receiving target or starting running back. The model may be assigning high value to kicking outcomes; that assumption needs to be stated and supported.
* The defensive backs rank unusually high: CB1 and CB2 outrank WR1, the tackles, and almost every other spot. Elite corners are extremely valuable, but a conventional overall ranking would usually put QB first and then place top pass rushers, tackles, receivers, and corners in a closer tier. The exact order depends heavily on the player and scheme.
* The table is about modeled contribution, not pay: A player’s salary reflects scarcity, bargaining leverage, contract timing, position-group depth, and the way teams allocate cap space—not just how many wins a model assigns. The NFL’s own 2025 top-paid list, for example, puts leading tackle and interior-line contracts well above the leading RB contract. NFL.com
A conventional blended ranking
If I combine typical on-field importance and market pay—rather than claiming this is a precise mathematical result—I’d use broad tiers:
1. QB
2. Top edge rusher, OT, WR1, and CB1 — the order varies by player and team
3. Other starting tackles, top defensive tackles, CB2, WR2
4. Starting interior linemen, safeties, linebackers, TE1
5. RB1 and kicker — close and context-dependent; RB1 generally contributes more snaps, while kicker has high leverage on scoring plays
6. WR3, RB2, TE2, SAM
So the table’s main idea—that roles contribute different amounts and in different ways—is reasonable. Its specific win values and ordering should be labeled as model output, not as an established conventional or salary ranking. I’d especially revisit CB1/CB2’s placement, the kicker above WR2/RB1, and the near-zero values for TE2 and SAM.
