## §1. The flex briefing — the whole story, from the start

### What flex is

Flex is playing a player out of his natural position, at a penalty. A wide receiver playing cornerback, a tight end playing defensive back, a defensive tackle playing fullback — each move costs something, measured in points off the lineup's value. The flex table is the list of those costs: 97 rules (now growing — see below), each one a (from-position → to-position) pair with a penalty of 0, 2, 6, or 12. A penalty of 0 means the move is free. A penalty of 12 means it is very expensive.

The flex table is the Owner's. He decides the costs. The table is a guide for the Architect (Claude) to understand how the Owner wants the engine to work — it is not the engine itself. The engine will eventually derive these prices from per-role skill profiles (see §1.7), but for now the table is hand-set by the Owner.

### The D-picks table (the current table's foundation)

The table was built from a formula called D (displacement-aware), at theta = 1.0, rounded to the nearest tier (0/2/6/12). D measures how much a position is worth when it displaces another player, using the engine's own slot weights. The Owner adopted D, then overrode it with his own rules wherever his judgment (from college/NFL conversion rates and football sense) disagreed with the formula. His rules supersede the formula.

The resulting table (built by Claude, `35ce4e98`) has 97 rules: 19 at 12, 32 at 6, 16 at 2, 30 at 0. Seven rules were removed entirely (FB>EDGE, FB>DT, FB>ILB, FB>OLB, CB>RB, DT>TE, EDGE>FS). EDGE>TE is −6, NOT removed.

Key facts about the table that matter for the UI:
- **FB is free.** Nine roles play fullback for free (RB, TE, OT, OG, C, DT, EDGE, ILB, OLB all go to FB at 0). There are few natural fullbacks and FB is the least important spot, so the Owner wants it easy on the user at the end of the draft.
- **WR>TE is 6** because a wide receiver can't block — he receives a lack-of-blocking penalty. TE>WR is 12. The asymmetry is the point: the cost comes from what the player *can't* do in the new spot.
- **QB>FS is 12.** A quarterback can play free safety, but it is expensive. This is a fun flex the Owner explicitly wants to keep possible (see §1.8).
- **WR>CB, WR>FS, WR>SS, WR>NICKEL, WR>DIME are all 2.** Wide receivers are good athletes, similar to defensive backs, so they move to DB cheaply. The reverse is steep (CB>WR 6, SS>WR 12, FS>WR 12) because defensive backs can't catch like receivers. The asymmetry is because receivers are better and paid more because they can catch.
- **MIKE> WILL is free (ILB>OLB 0), WILL>MIKE is 6 (OLB>ILB 6).** The better linebacker (MIKE) drops to WILL for nothing; the lesser linebacker (WILL) moving up to MIKE costs 6.
- **ILB>ILB and OLB>OLB are in the table at 0 but do nothing** (the engine returns 0 for a role at its own destination before it looks at the table). The Owner keeps them because the terms are vague: ILB = MIKE and OLB = WILL. SAM is separate and lesser-valued because SAMs come off the field for the nickel often.
- **EDGE>OLB and EDGE>DT stayed at 0.** An edge rusher moving to outside linebacker (a 3-4 EDGE to OLB) is natural and free. This was intended.

### The 9 corrections (already built)

After the table was built, Claude flagged that EDGE flex was nearly free (EDGE>DT/OLB/ILB/OG/OT/RB/FB/NICKEL all 0), and the Owner agreed several rules were too cheap. Nine rules moved (Owner overrides, NOT formula-derived):

- To 6 (8 rules): EDGE>ILB, EDGE>OG, EDGE>OT, EDGE>RB, EDGE>NICKEL (all 0→6); DT>C (0→6, a DT cannot snap); DT>OG (2→6, matches OG>DT); EDGE>C (2→6, an EDGE cannot snap, consistent with the EDGE cluster).
- To 2 (1 rule): OG>C (0→2 — a guard playing center costs 2, because snapping is a skill).
- C>OG stays 6. The Owner confirmed this: a center moving to guard costs 6.

After the corrections the table is **19 at 12, 40 at 6, 15 at 2, 23 at 0 — still 97 rules.** Claude built this and pushed it as `728b2c53` (tests 290/290, flexcheck and flexdl5 pass). The 3-4 base total came out at 2400, not the 3600 the prompt predicted, because the lineup finds a cheaper arrangement (a stand-in at one inside spot, the natural FB at TE3, a TE at DIME, and the spare DT playing FB for free). The TE3 weight is 50, not 100.

### The Owner's answers to Claude's 6 questions (verbatim reasoning — store this)

Claude asked six questions about flexes that didn't make sense to him. The Owner answered (these are his words and his reasons — they are the logic the engine must eventually embody):

1. **Nine roles play FB for free.** "Yes that's fine. There are few natural FBs and it is the least important spot. We want to make it easy on the user at the end of the draft."
2. **The C/OG direction is inverted (C>OG 6, OG>C 2).** "My logic is that guards are better athletes and are weighed more favorably by the engine in terms of impact. The cost to snapping is less than being a better athlete and more one on one matchups with the 3-technique." (A guard moving to center costs only the snapping skill; a center moving to guard costs more because the center is the lesser athlete and guard is more one-on-one against the 3-technique.)
3. **Tight ends cost less than linebackers at defensive back.** See §1.5 below — this one changed twice and is now settled.
4. **Receivers to DB are cheap, the reverse is steep.** "My logic is that DBs and WRs are similar athletes but WRs are better and paid more because they can catch."
5. **The edge rusher moves left alone (EDGE>OLB and EDGE>DT at 0).** "Yes, that was intended."
6. **ILB>ILB and OLB>OLB do nothing.** "I like those rules just because there is some vagueness of terms. ILB=MIKE and OLB=WILL. SAM is separate and lesser valued because they come off the field for the nickel often."

### The skill-profile IDEA (the future of flex)

Claude proposed, and the Owner supports, replacing the 97 hand-set rules with a per-role skill profile: each role gets scores for blocking, coverage, snapping, rushing (and similar), and flex prices are produced from one place instead of 97 hand-set rules. The Owner does not think the rules will keep growing, but he thinks this type of thinking should be embedded in the engine. His flex table is merely a guide for the Architect to understand how he wants the engine to work. This is an engine direction (Claude's), not a Slice 3 task. Do not build it. But know it exists, because it is where the flex system is heading.

### Why flexing stays (the fun principle)

Part of why the Owner is keeping flexing despite its complexity is that it is FUN for the user. They will want to see their second-favorite QB playing FS even if it is less efficient. Or the user can wonder how Tee Higgins would play at CB. Flexing isn't just for accuracy (and the Owner wants it to be logical and accurate) but also for fun. The board chrome should make that kind of play visible and delightful — let the user try the weird flex, not just the optimal one.
